## What is the Jevons paradox?

In 1865 William Stanley Jevons noticed something odd: as steam engines got more efficient with coal, Britain burned *more* coal, not less. Cheaper energy made new uses worth it, and demand exploded. Software is having its coal moment. When building something costs a tenth of what it used to, all the projects that were "not worth it" suddenly are: the internal tool, the one-off migration, the app for a niche of a niche.

## What happens when typing gets cheap?

Writing code was never the whole job, but it was the visible, expensive part. Now an agent types faster than you, never gets bored, and works at 3 a.m. So the backlog of "someday" ideas becomes "this afternoon." Companies won't build the same software with fewer people; they'll build far more software. The catch: someone still has to decide what's worth building and whether it actually works.

## What is scarce now?

Eliyahu Goldratt's *The Goal*: a system moves at the speed of its constraint. Making any other part faster only piles work up in front of that constraint. Typing is no longer the constraint. Deciding what to build, and knowing the result works, is.

That's the thread through the rest of this book. Every chapter is really about one of those two scarce things — clear intent going in, trustworthy verification coming out.
